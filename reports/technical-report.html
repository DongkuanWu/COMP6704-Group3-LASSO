GROUP 3 · CONVEX OPTIMIZATION

Convex optimization for sparse signal reconstruction

Technical report · A paired comparison of ISTA, FISTA and ADMM

Abstract. How much of a solver’s advantage survives when the comparison moves from iteration count to elapsed time? On 200 synthetic problem instances, all three solvers reached the specified thresholds and produced closely matching objective values. At baseline, ADMM averaged 40.5 updates compared with 95 for FISTA and 148 for ISTA. The explicit inverse used by ADMM accounted for about 98% of its measured runtime. FISTA averaged 1.74 ms and ISTA 2.12 ms. Recovery quality depended strongly on the measurements and regularization. Increasing the penalty reduced solve time on the tested data while increasing reconstruction error. These findings characterize the implementations and machine used for this benchmark.

Code, data and reproduction instructions: https://github.com/DongkuanWu/COMP6704-Group3-LASSO.

1. Introduction

A sensor often records mixtures of a signal’s components. With a fixed measurement budget, several signals can fit the same observations equally well. Sparsity provides a way to choose among them. In our baseline example, a sensor returns 96 numbers for a signal with 192 coefficients, of which only ten are nonzero. Knowing the support would reduce the number of unknowns substantially. In this experiment the estimator must infer both the active coefficients and their values.

We ask three questions. Do different optimization procedures reach comparable objective values? Does finishing in fewer updates also reduce wall-clock time? And once the solver has converged, how much does the reconstructed signal depend on the amount of information and the regularization strength? The last question matters because excellent numerical agreement between solvers can coexist with substantial signal error.

2. Model and numerical methods

Let A ∈ ℝm×n be a known measurement matrix, x⋆ ∈ ℝn an unknown sparse signal and ε additive noise. The observations satisfy b = Ax⋆ + ε. When m < n, A has a nontrivial null space. Moving a candidate signal along that space leaves its predicted observations unchanged. We use the LASSO objective to favor reconstructions with small ℓ₁ norm.

minx ∈ ℝⁿ F(x) = ½‖Ax − b‖₂² + λ‖x‖₁,   λ = α‖Aᵀb‖∞.

The squared loss has Hessian AᵀA, which is positive semidefinite. The ℓ₁ term is convex and nonsmooth at zero. Their sum is convex and each local minimum is global. Solution uniqueness requires additional conditions. Increasing λ places greater weight on coefficient shrinkage. We evaluate objective agreement separately from relative reconstruction error, ‖x − x⋆‖₂ / ‖x⋆‖₂.

ISTA takes a gradient step of length 1/L, where L = ‖A‖₂², then applies componentwise soft thresholding at λ/L. For a scalar v, Sτ(v) = sign(v) max(|v| − τ, 0). FISTA applies the same update at a momentum-extrapolated point. Both use dense matrix–vector products per step. Acceleration can reduce the number of steps while increasing the work per step. See Beck and Teboulle (2009).

ADMM splits the variable into x and z with x = z. Each cycle solves the quadratic x-subproblem, soft-thresholds x + u to obtain z and updates the scaled dual variable u. The implementation forms (AᵀA + ρI)−1 once per timed run with ρ = 1, then reuses it at every iteration. It returns z. This choice adds dense matrix preparation to every run, including repeated timings of the same instance. Factorization and reuse across related solves would change that cost. See Boyd et al. (2011).

3. Experimental design

Each seed generates a Gaussian sensing matrix with unit-norm columns, a uniformly sampled support without replacement, random signs, and coefficient magnitudes uniform on [0.8, 1.2]. Independent Gaussian measurement noise has standard deviation σ. Baseline parameters are (m, n, s, σ, α) = (96, 192, 10, 0.02, 0.05). We vary m to 64 or 128, s to 5 or 24, σ to 0 or 0.08, and α to 0.01 or 0.15. A final size test doubles m, n and s together. Within a setting all three solvers see exactly the same A, b and λ.

The shared optimality check is ‖L[x − Sλ/L(x − ∇f(x)/L)]‖∞ / max(1, ‖Aᵀb‖∞) ≤ 10−4, where f is the squared-loss term. Checks occur at iteration 1, every tenth iteration and the 1,200-iteration cap. ADMM also checks normalized primal and dual residuals. Reported iteration counts therefore measure the first successful scheduled check.

We ran ten settings with twenty seeds, three solvers and five timing repeats, yielding 3,000 timed runs. For each instance we shuffled the fifteen solver/repeat jobs, timed elapsed seconds with Python’s performance counter, took the median time for each solver, and averaged those medians across seeds. Each interval uses 2,000 percentile bootstrap resamples of the twenty instances. For FISTA versus ISTA we first subtract their medians on each shared instance, then resample these paired differences. The five repeats measure timing variability for one problem.

Shared data generation and computation of L occur before the timer. The proximal solvers also form Aᵀb before timing, while ADMM includes Aᵀb and inverse setup. The implementation records elapsed time at the last convergence check. Machine load, BLAS settings and cache state can influence these short timings. No dedicated warm-up, affinity control or independent machine replication was recorded. The pilot and FISTA’s separate supplementary study are outside the pooled results below.

4. Objective agreement and convergence

All 3,000 runs reached their thresholds. Across the 200 instances, the largest difference between the three final objective values on the same instance was 2.30 × 10−6. At baseline, mean relative errors were 8.47% for ISTA, 8.46% for FISTA and 8.46% for ADMM. Agreement at both levels makes the timing comparison interpretable. It does not establish exact support recovery or a certificate of distance to the true optimizer.

5. Iteration counts and elapsed time

ADMM used 40.5 iterations on average, compared with 95 for FISTA and 148 for ISTA. Yet ADMM averaged 48.83 ms [29.70, 71.97], against 1.74 ms [1.56, 1.97] for FISTA and 2.12 ms [1.84, 2.45] for ISTA. To attribute cost consistently, we select the complete run with median total time for each seed. The sum of its setup times divided by the sum of its total times is 97.8% for baseline ADMM. A workload that reuses one matrix across many solves could amortize that preparation.

FISTA had a lower mean time in nine settings. In the baseline setting, it saved 0.378 ms on average, with a paired interval for the saving of [0.110, 0.637] ms. With 128 measurements, it averaged 73 iterations against ISTA’s 91, yet took 0.172 ms longer [0.014, 0.350]. At weaker regularization its saving reached 4.161 ms. These comparisons are exploratory and use no adjustment for multiple testing. The observed reversal establishes that update counts alone are insufficient to predict elapsed time. Identifying the precise overhead behind the reversal would require profiling.

6. Recovery quality under changing conditions

Increasing the measurement count from 64 to 96 reduced error from 11.57% to 8.46%. Increasing it to 128 reduced error further to 8.26%. At five nonzeros the error was 7.08%, compared with 19.68% at 24 nonzeros. The comparisons describe the signal distributions defined by these settings. Changing dimensions can also change the random draws, so a common seed across settings does not necessarily preserve the underlying signal.

Holding the baseline data fixed and increasing α from 0.01 to 0.05 to 0.15 reduced FISTA time from 2.67 to 1.74 to 1.32 ms. Over the same three points, recovery error rose from 5.26% to 8.46% to 24.43%. This is a practical reason to evaluate reconstruction after convergence. The fastest setting also had the largest shrinkage bias in this sweep. With zero noise and baseline regularization, mean error remained 8.22%. Increasing σ to 0.08 raised it to 20.10%. Because λ depends on b, the noise comparison also permits λ to vary. No held-out validation set was used to select α.

7. Conclusion and limits

On these synthetic dense problems, the three implementations obtained comparable objective values and recovery errors. FISTA usually reduced elapsed time relative to ISTA. ADMM required fewer updates but spent most of its measured time building an explicit inverse. Stronger regularization reduced runtime while worsening signal recovery in the baseline sweep. A solver evaluation should report objective accuracy, reconstruction error and elapsed time together.

The evidence covers ten settings, one recorded software environment, fixed ADMM ρ, and small Gaussian matrices. To test how much of the ADMM timing gap is attributable to implementation, repeat the same paired benchmark with Cholesky solves and separate cold from reused setup. To isolate noise effects, fix λ across noise levels. Larger structured matrices, real signals, and measurements across several machines would address different questions about generality.
